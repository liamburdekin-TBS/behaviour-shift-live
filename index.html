THE MINDSET
BEHIND THE RESPONSE.

PMA is not pretending everything is fine. Difficult situations are still difficult. The professional skill is choosing the mindset that gives you the best chance of responding usefully.

Hope

Pressure Profiles · Turn the lens around

PRESSURE DOESN'T JUST CHANGE THE ROOM.
IT CHANGES US.

A Pressure Profile is the version of your communication, thinking and behaviour that becomes more likely when demand starts exceeding capacity.

Pressure does not create everything we see. It amplifies tendencies that are already there.
NOT A PERSONALITY TEST

This is not a label and it is not a diagnosis. It is a reflective lens for noticing patterns.

NOT GOOD OR BAD

Every profile begins with useful strengths. Pressure can make those strengths narrower or less flexible if we stop noticing how we are using them.

THE ADULT MATTERS

Your breathing, tone, pace, posture and decisions become part of the environment the young person is responding to.

BEFORE I REGULATE SOMEBODY ELSE, I HAVE TO NOTICE ME.

Pressure Profiles · What each profile brings

EVERY PROFILE BRINGS
SOMETHING USEFUL.

These are not good or bad profiles. Each one describes useful instincts you may naturally lean on. The value is knowing what you bring — and one thing to stay mindful of when pressure rises.

THE CONTROLLER

Brings: clarity, structure, consistency, decisiveness and confidence holding boundaries.
Be mindful of: leaving enough room for curiosity as well as clarity.

THE RESCUER

Brings: warmth, empathy, reassurance, connection and a strong instinct to notice distress.
Be mindful of: supporting without stepping in so quickly that the young person loses space to do for themselves.

THE REACTOR

Brings: passion, energy, courage, emotional investment and willingness to act when something matters.
Be mindful of: giving yourself enough pause to choose how that energy comes into the room.

THE BREATHER

Brings: calm, restraint, breathing space, patience and an instinct not to feed unnecessary conflict.
Be mindful of: making sure the breathing space has a purpose and that important conversations are returned to when the moment is right.

THE PROBLEM SOLVER

Brings: logic, organisation, practical thinking, resourcefulness and an ability to create a route forward.
Be mindful of: understanding the person before moving too quickly to the solution.

THE REGULATOR

Brings: steadiness, observation, patience, emotional containment and an ability to reduce heat in the room.
Be mindful of: making sure calm still feels present, connected and ready to act when needed.

Strong teams need different profiles. The aim is not to change yours — it is to know your strengths and use them deliberately.

Unpack the scenario

BEFORE WE MANAGE THE HOUSE,
ORGANISE THE TEAM.

There is not one magic answer. There are, however, some things that make the next ten minutes much safer and clearer.

Communicate

Share what each person knows. Do not let three staff act on three different versions of the morning.

Set priorities

Decide what needs immediate attention, what can wait and what can be reduced by changing the environment.

Clarify roles

Who is leading? Who is supporting breakfast? Who is giving space? Role clarity reduces contradiction.

Regulate first

If the team is rushed, loud or reactive, that becomes part of the environment the young people are waking into.

Sometimes the first behaviour-management decision is not about the young person at all. It is about what the adults do together.

Section 04 · The Human Animal
04

READ THE PERSON BEFORE THE PROBLEM.

Before we ask for explanations or reach for tools, we learn to notice what people may already be communicating without words.

WE’RE NOW MOVING INTO: THE HUMAN ANIMAL
33 / 80
The Human Animal · Desmond Morris

BEFORE WORDS,
WE WERE READING EACH OTHER.

Zoologist and author Desmond Morris explored human behaviour as something we can watch as well as listen to. In The Human Animal, he looked across cultures at gesture, posture, ritual, status and the signals human beings constantly send.

WE SIGNAL

Face, hands, shoulders, distance, movement, breathing, pace and silence all communicate.

WE INTERPRET

Our brain gives those signals meaning — often before anybody has explained what is happening.

WE CAN MISREAD

Culture, trauma, personality, neurodiversity, relationships and context can change what the same signal means.

THE HUMAN ANIMAL LESSON: NOTICE THE SIGNAL. STAY CURIOUS ABOUT THE MEANING.
Reference Desmond Morris and The Human Animal. The point is observation, not turning body language into a fixed dictionary.
↑33 / 80↓
34 / 80
The Human Animal · Same species, different signals

BODY LANGUAGE HAS
A CULTURE TOO.

Morris’s work is useful because it shows both sides at once: humans share a huge amount, but the way we express and read signals is shaped by where and how we learned them.

THE INDIAN HEAD WOBBLE

A side-to-side head wobble can communicate acknowledgement, understanding, agreement or something more ambiguous depending on context. A British observer could easily read the movement differently.

THE HANDSHAKE

Firmness, duration, eye contact and when contact ends can carry different social meanings. In some settings a handshake is a quick greeting; in others it is part of a longer ritual of trust or agreement.

PERSONAL SPACE

How close we stand, whether we face someone directly, and how much eye contact feels respectful can vary between people, cultures and situations.

So when a young person looks away, moves back or will not face us, the movement is real — our first interpretation might not be.

We are not learning a dictionary of gestures. We are learning to notice before deciding.

The behaviour cycle

WHAT HAPPENS
BEFORE THE INCIDENT?

TRIGGER

Something changes: a demand, memory, loss of control, peer reaction or perceived threat.

MEANING

The child interprets it: “I’m unsafe”, “I’m being embarrassed”, “Nobody listens”, “I must win”.

STATE

The body moves into fight, flight, freeze, shut-down or high alert.

BEHAVIOUR

The visible action becomes the part adults often respond to first.

ADULT RESPONSE

Our tone, timing and frame can reduce the threat — or add another trigger.

Behaviour management starts earlier than the behaviour.

Framing · Oren Klaff → Behaviour Shift

CHANGE HOW THE SITUATION
IS BEING SEEN.

Oren Klaff’s work on frame control comes from high-stakes pitching: the frame is the meaning people accept about what an interaction is really about. Behaviour Shift borrows one useful idea — if the current frame only offers conflict, help create a frame with a safer route forward.

FRAME
THE YOUNG PERSON’S FRAME

“I’ve got no data. Staff are stopping me getting what I need. If I kick off hard enough, they’ll have to top it up.”

The situation feels immediate, unfair and like a power contest.
THE BEHAVIOUR SHIFT FRAME

“The data matters. Smashing the house does not get you closer to it. There is a route to earning the top-up without making tonight worse.”

The same need is now connected to choice, control and a workable next step.
Framing is not talking somebody out of their feelings. It is helping them see a different set of options.

THEY ARE NOT JUST HEARING
OUR LOGIC.

1 · WANT

“Is getting your data sorted what you actually want?”

2 · IMPACT

“If you smash the house, does that move you closer to it or further away?”

3 · DIRECTION

“Do you want this situation bigger or smaller?”

4 · OPTION

“What could you do now that keeps the route to the top-up open?”

“Which route are you choosing?”

The conclusion becomes easier to own because they have participated in building it.
Socratic questioning is not a trick and not an interrogation. At high arousal: fewer words first. Questions come when the young person can actually use them.

Boundaries

A BOUNDARY IS
INFORMATION, NOT REVENGE.

“I know leaving feels unfair.”

2 · LIMIT

Escalation

WHEN THE BRAIN GOES INTO THREAT,
USE FEWER WORDS.

LOW AROUSAL

Conversation, curiosity, humour and collaborative problem-solving may be available.

RISING

Reduce demands, slow your voice, create space, name one next action.

CRISIS

Prioritise immediate safety, follow the plan and policy, minimise language and audience.

RECOVERY

Do not rush the debrief. Water, space, regulation and dignity may come first.

The right intervention at the wrong stage can still be the wrong intervention.

Scenario lab

THE BEDROOM DOOR
SCENARIO.

A young person has been told their phone will be held overnight following repeated unsafe contact. They slam the bedroom door, shout that staff are thieves and begin kicking the wall.
What is the surface behaviour?

Shouting, accusation, property damage and refusal.

What might sit underneath?

Loss of control, panic about connection, shame, fear of exclusion, or expectation that adults take without listening.

What frame could trap staff?

“They must learn who is in charge.” This can turn safety into a dominance contest.

What is the immediate goal?

Reduce risk and arousal while holding the phone boundary and preserving a route back to dialogue.

Facilitator: Ask pairs to produce no more than two opening sentences. Brevity exposes whether the response is actually clear.
